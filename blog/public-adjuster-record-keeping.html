Public adjuster record keeping requirements

By ClaimHive · Updated Nov 1, 2026 · For owners of public adjusting firms

Under the NAIC Public Adjuster Licensing Model Act, a public adjuster keeps a complete record of each claim, covering ten named items, for at least five years after the transaction ends [1]. Your state may use different wording or a different period, so treat the model act as a starting list and check your own statute.

Most firm owners we talk to know this rule exists. The harder part is that the records live in five places: a shared drive, an inbox, a phone camera roll, an accounting tool and a filing cabinet. When a regulator, a carrier's counsel or a former client asks for the file, someone has to rebuild it by hand. This post walks through what the model act asks for and how to set up a file so you are not rebuilding it later.

What records does a public adjuster have to keep?

The model act lists ten records for each transaction [1]:

  1. Name of the insured.
  2. Date, location and amount of the loss.
  3. A copy of the contract between the public adjuster and the insured.
  4. Name of the insurer, plus the amount, expiration date and number of each policy that applies to the loss.
  5. An itemized statement of the insured's recoveries.
  6. An itemized statement of all compensation the public adjuster received, from any source, in connection with the loss.
  7. A register of all money received, deposited, disbursed or withdrawn in connection with the transaction, including fees, trust account transfers and disbursements, and interest-bearing account activity.
  8. Name of the public adjuster who executed the contract.
  9. Name of the attorney representing the insured, if any, and the names of the insurer's claim representatives.
  10. Evidence of financial responsibility, in the format the insurance commissioner prescribes.

Notice what is missing from that list: photos, estimates, emails and inspection notes. The model act focuses on the contract and the money. Your own risk posture is a separate question, and most firms keep far more than the minimum.

How long does a public adjuster keep claim records?

The model act says at least five years after the transaction terminates [1]. That clock starts when the claim ends. A claim that took two years to settle means the file stays around for seven years or more from the day the policyholder first called you.

States adopt model acts in their own words. Before you set a retention policy, read your state's public adjuster statute or ask your department of insurance. If you hold licenses in several states, the longest period you face is the simplest one to adopt firm-wide.

Who can ask to see the records?

Under the model act, the records must be open to the insurance commissioner for examination at any time [1]. In practice, firm owners also field requests from policyholders, successor adjusters and attorneys. A file you can export in one pass is far less painful than one you assemble from memory.

What should a complete claim file look like in practice?

A workable structure mirrors the ten items above and adds the working papers your adjusters already create. One layout that holds up:

FolderWhat goes in itModel act item
ContractSigned PA contract, any amendments, the policyholder's contact record1, 3, 8
PolicyDeclarations pages and policy numbers for every policy that applies4
LossDate, location, cause and claimed amount of the loss2
PartiesCarrier claim representatives, any attorney for the policyholder9
MoneyEvery payment in and out, fee calculations, trust account entries5, 6, 7
ComplianceBond or other proof of financial responsibility for the period10
Working papersPhotos, estimates, correspondence, inspection notesNot required by the model act

The point of the table is the third column. If an item number has no folder, you have a gap.

Where do claim files usually break down?

From conversations with PA firm owners, the same weak spots come up again and again:

  • Fee statements live in accounting software and never get copied into the claim file. Items 6 and 7 are the ones most likely to be incomplete.
  • Desk adjusters change mid-claim. If nobody logs the new name, item 9 is wrong by the time the claim closes.
  • Photos and texts sit on an adjuster's phone. When that adjuster leaves the firm, part of the file leaves too.
  • Firms that switch systems often move open claims and leave closed ones behind in the old tool, which still sit inside the retention window.

How do you audit your files this quarter?

You do not need new software to start. Pick ten closed claims from the last five years, at random. For each one, check the ten model act items and mark each as present, partial or missing. Then count.

  • If a single item is missing across most files, fix the process that creates it, usually a closing checklist.
  • If misses are spread out, the problem is ownership. Name one person who signs off that a file is complete before it closes.
  • If closed files from an old system are missing entirely, export them now while you still have access.

Repeat the audit each quarter with a fresh sample. It takes an afternoon and tells you where you stand before anyone else asks.

FAQ

Does the model act apply in my state?

Only if your state adopted it, and states often change the text when they do. The NAIC model act is a template [1]. Your state statute controls.

Do I need to keep photos and estimates?

The model act's list does not name them [1]. Many firms keep them anyway because they support the claim if it is disputed later. Set a written policy and apply it the same way to every file.

Can records be kept electronically?

The model act's record retention section does not address format [1]. Check your state's rule before you scan and shred paper files.

This post is general information, not legal advice. Retention rules differ by state. Talk with a licensed attorney or your state department of insurance before you set a records policy.
